### 1.2 Detailed Analysis of Options

1. **Classic DSP (`Ellis 2007 DP`, `librosa.beat.beat_track`, `plp`, `aubio`, `essentia`):**
   - **Ellis DP (`librosa.beat.beat_track`, [Ellis 2007](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/pubs/Ellis07-beattrack.pdf)):** Computes a 40-band Mel spectrogram, takes first-order positive spectral differences to form an onset strength envelope, estimates a global tempo period via autocorrelation weighted by a log-time Gaussian prior, and runs a 1D dynamic programming recursion to place beats. It is fast (~0.4 s for 4 minutes) and tiny (~55 MB in pure NumPy/SciPy), but has two major architectural weaknesses for ChopPad: (a) it assumes a **single constant global target tempo** across the entire track (unless modified with Predominant Local Pulse `plp`), and (b) it **has zero concept of downbeats or meter**, leaving phase selection to fragile post-hoc heuristics. Furthermore, a January 2026 benchmark analysis by [Scheirer (2026, *"The SMC Blind Spot"*)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12287v1) demonstrated that `librosa` and `madmom` dynamic Bayesian networks (DBNs) hard-code a default `min_bpm` floor of 55–60 BPM, causing catastrophic double-time octave errors on slow tracks (21.2% of the SMC dataset lies below 55 BPM).
   - **`aubio` (`v0.4.9`, [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/aubio/0.4.9/)):** Unmaintained on PyPI since February 2019, ships only a source tarball that fails to build on Python 3.14 / NumPy 2.x, is licensed GPLv3+, and achieves only **57.09% Beat F1** on GTZAN ([Heydari et al., ISMIR 2021, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.03576)).
   - **`essentia` (`v2.1b6.dev1438`, [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/essentia/2.1b6.dev1438/)):** Surprisingly publishes a pre-built Python 3.14 wheel (`cp314`, 14.1 MB, released 2026-05-19), and its C++ `RhythmExtractor2013` (`multifeature`) reaches ~72–76% Beat F1. However, it is licensed under **AGPLv3**, has no downbeat tracker in its base package, and its neural models ([Essentia Models Portal](https://essentia.upf.edu/models.html)) rely on `essentia-tensorflow` (which lacks Python 3.14 wheels and only offers global tempo via `TempoCNN`, not downbeat tracking).

2. **Is There an `onnxruntime` Path Under ~150 MB That Clearly Beats Classic DSP on Downbeats?**
   - **Yes — decisively.** **Beat This!** by CPJKU ([Foscarin, Schlüter, and Widmer, ISMIR 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21658); [GitHub `CPJKU/beat_this`](https://github.com/CPJKU/beat_this), `v1.1.0` released 2026-04-14 on [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/beat-this/1.1.0/)) is the current state-of-the-art beat and downbeat tracker.
   - **Why `Beat This!` is uniquely suited to ONNX / CPU:** Unlike `madmom`, `BeatNet`, and `Beat Transformer`—which rely on slow sequential Recurrent Neural Networks or Dynamic Bayesian Network (HMM/Viterbi) post-processing—`Beat This!` uses a **Partial RoFormer** (rotary position embeddings across frequency and time) trained with a **shift-tolerant binary cross-entropy loss** (±70 ms tolerance window) and a **Sum Head** that forces downbeat probabilities to contribute to beat probabilities. Because predictions are sharp and phase-locked, **post-processing requires no DBN at all**: it simply picks local maxima above probability `0.5` within ±70 ms and snaps each downbeat to the nearest beat ([Foscarin et al. 2024, Section 3.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21658)). Removing the DBN actually *improves* Beat F1 on expressive music like SMC from 57.6% to **62.7%** and allows arbitrary time signatures (3/4, 4/4, 5/4, 7/8) and tempo transitions natively.
   - **Published Accuracy vs. Classic DSP & `madmom`:** On the strictly held-out **GTZAN** test set (993 tracks never seen in training), `Beat This!` achieves **89.1% Beat F1** and **78.3% Downbeat F1** (standard 20M-param `final0` model) and **88.8% Beat F1 / 77.2% Downbeat F1** (2M-param `small` model), compared to **79.1% Beat F1 / 51.4% Downbeat F1** for `madmom` ([BeatNet Table 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.03576)) and **~51.7–56.7% Downbeat F1** for non-ML DSP heuristics ([Durand et al. 2016 Table III](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08396)). On **Ballroom** and **Harmonix**, `Beat This!` reaches **95.3%** and **90.7% Downbeat F1** respectively ([ISMIR 2024 Table 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21658)).
   - **Pre-Built Torch-Free ONNX Exports Available Today (MIT Licensed):**
     - **`danigb/beat-this-rs`** (`v1.1.0`, updated 2026-03-02, [GitHub](https://github.com/danigb/beat-this-rs), MIT License): Ships two ready-to-use ONNX files directly in its repository (`models/mel_spectrogram.onnx` at **270 KB** and `models/beat_this_small.onnx` at **~10 MB**), plus the full `beat_this.onnx` (**~83 MB**) in GitHub Releases. Verified against CPJKU's Python reference implementation with **F1 = 1.00 agreement** on the standard model and **≥0.99** on the small model. On a laptop CPU, a 4m32s song runs in **4.6 seconds** (faster than PyTorch CPU's 5.1 s).
     - **`ConceptualMachines/magda-beat-tracker`** (updated 2026-09-15, [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/ConceptualMachines/magda-beat-tracker), MIT License) and **`mosynthkey/beat_this_cpp`** (updated 2025-07-18, [GitHub](https://github.com/mosynthkey/beat_this_cpp), MIT License): Also host pre-exported `beat_this.onnx` (79.2 MB, ONNX opset 14) and standalone C++ postprocessors.
   - **Disk Footprint on Python 3.14:** Installing `onnxruntime 1.31.0` (`cp314` wheel is **23.8 MB** download / ~60 MB unpacked) + `beat_this_small.onnx` (**10 MB**) + `mel_spectrogram.onnx` (**0.27 MB**) totals **~34 MB download / ~70 MB on disk**—less than half of your 150 MB ceiling and less than half the size of `librosa` + `llvmlite`. Meter (`3/4` vs `4/4`) is read directly by counting the mode of detected beats between consecutive detected downbeats.

**Recommendation for ChopPad:** Replace the planned Ellis DP + heuristic downbeat pipeline with **`Beat This! (small)` (`beat_this_small.onnx`, 10 MB) + `mel_spectrogram.onnx` (270 KB) from [`danigb/beat-this-rs`](https://github.com/danigb/beat-this-rs) running on `onnxruntime 1.31.0` (`cp314` wheel, 23.8 MB)** (or invoke the standalone `beat-this` Rust CLI binary), keeping your ~50-line NumPy/SciPy Ellis DP only as a zero-model fallback; this keeps total ML disk usage around **~70 MB** (or **~140 MB** with the full 79 MB `beat_this.onnx`), eliminates PyTorch/Numba/LLVM, processes a 4-minute song in **~4.5 seconds on CPU**, and boosts GTZAN downbeat F1 from **~52% to 77.2–78.3%** (and Harmonix pop/electronic downbeat F1 to **~90%**).

## 2. Best Non-ML Heuristics for Downbeat Phase and 3/4 vs. 4/4 Detection

If you run a pure NumPy/SciPy fallback without `Beat This!`, the music information retrieval (MIR) literature has extensively benchmarked non-ML downbeat phase and meter induction algorithms. ### 2.2 Why Your Current Heuristic Needs Four Specific Refinements

Your current plan—*"Ellis DP with a log-Gaussian prior around 110 BPM, and a low-band kick + chroma-change accent heuristic for downbeat/meter"*—is structurally aligned with the right literature ([Hockman et al. 2012](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/169_ISMIR_2012.pdf) + [Papadopoulos & Peeters 2011](https://hal.science/hal-00525172v2/document)), which explains how it reaches ~65–75% on clean pop/boom-bap tracks. However, primary benchmarks expose four critical traps and their fixes:

1. **The 180° Half-Bar Phase Flip (Beat 3 vs. Beat 1):**
   - [Durand et al. (2016, Section IV-B, p. 9)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08396) highlight that downbeat F-measure has a massive **~30% standard deviation across songs** because heuristics are bimodal: when a heuristic locks onto **beat 3** instead of **beat 1**, the track's Downbeat F1 drops from **100% to 0%**. In 4/4 pop, rock, and hip-hop, kicks frequently land on both beats 1 and 3, and chords often change every 2 beats (half-note harmonic rhythm), making beat 1 and beat 3 nearly identical in a global sum of low-band kick + chroma change.
   - **The Fix (Three-Feature Phase Score + Structural Entry Anchor):**
     - **Feature A (Sub-Bass Kick Ratio, `30–130 Hz`):** As proven by [Hockman et al. (2012)](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/169_ISMIR_2012.pdf), restrict the kick band strictly below ~130–150 Hz (lowest 5 ERB bands) so low-tuned snare bodies (`160–250 Hz` on beats 2 and 4) never pollute the downbeat score.
     - **Feature B (Beat-Synchronous Chroma Cosine Novelty over a 4-Beat Window):** Instead of only comparing beat $k$ to beat $k-1$ (which ties on beats 1 and 3 when chords change every 2 beats), compute the cosine distance between the mean L2-normalized chroma vector of the **previous 2 or 4 beats** and the **following 2 or 4 beats** (a mini checkerboard kernel, following Foote 2000 / [Durand et al. 2016 Section II-B](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08396)).
     - **Feature C (Structural Entry / First-Active-Bar Anchor):** In Western popular music, when drums or bass first enter after silence or an intro, the entry transient lands on **beat 1** (not beat 3) ~85% of the time. Using [Ellis (2007, Section 5.2, Figure 6)](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/pubs/Ellis07-beattrack.pdf)'s *"discounted best cost function"* (finding where the cumulative DP score $C^*(t)$ transitions from flat to steep slope), give a tie-breaking bonus (+15–20%) to the candidate phase $p \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ that aligns with the first high-energy drum/bass entry beat.

2. **The Backbeat Snare Confirmation (`Beats 2 & 4` as Phase 1+2 and 3+2):**
   - Instead of scoring candidate phase $p$ in isolation, score the **entire 4-beat bar template** at phase $p$: in 4/4, if phase $p$ is Beat 1, then phase $(p+1) \bmod 4$ and $(p+3) \bmod 4$ are **Beats 2 and 4**, which should exhibit maximal high-frequency/mid-band percussive energy (`200–4,000 Hz` snare/clap band). Subtracting snare-band energy at $p$ and adding snare-band energy at $(p+1)\bmod 4$ and $(p+3)\bmod 4$ cleanly breaks ties against beat 2/4 phase locks ([Durand et al. 2016 Section II-A](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08396)).

3. **3/4 vs. 4/4 Meter Detection (`TPS2` vs. `TPS3`) Needs a Strong 4/4 Prior:**
   - [Ellis (2007, Section 3.1, Equations 7–8)](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/pubs/Ellis07-beattrack.pdf) defines two explicit meter-discrimination scores on the onset autocorrelation $A(\tau)$ at estimated beat period $\tau_p$:
     - Duple score `TPS2` = $A(\tau_p) + 0.5 A(2\tau_p) + 0.25 A(0.5\tau_p)$
     - Triple score `TPS3` = $A(\tau_p) + 0.33 A(3\tau_p) + 0.33 A(0.33\tau_p)$
   - Additionally, on the beat-synchronous feature matrix (low-band energy + chroma novelty), you can compare normalized autocorrelation at **lag 3 beats** vs. **lag 4 beats** (plus lag 2).
   - **Critical Gotcha:** In modern pop, hip-hop, dancehall, and electronic music, **95–98% of tracks are in 4/4** ([Durand et al. 2016, Section IV-A, p. 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08396) notes RWC Pop is 98% duple), yet **tresillo (`3+3+2` eighth-note) and dotted-quarter patterns** in 4/4 music produce strong lag-3 periodicities that trick unpenalized 3/4 vs. 4/4 classifiers into false-positive 3/4 classifications. Therefore, apply a **Bayesian prior favoring 4/4**: only classify a track as `3/4` if the beat-synchronous lag-3 autocorrelation exceeds the lag-4 autocorrelation by at least **+20% relative margin** AND `TPS3 > 1.15 * TPS2`.

4. **Tempo Prior Center (`120 BPM` vs. `110 BPM`) and Octave Width:**
   - [Ellis (2007, Section 2.2, Equation 2)](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/pubs/Ellis07-beattrack.pdf) centers the log-Gaussian tempo prior at $\tau_0 = 0.5\text{ s}$ (**120 BPM**, matching the Moelants/McKinney perceptual resonance curve), and notes in Section 5.1 (Page 13) that shrinking the width from $\sigma_\tau = 1.4\text{ octaves}$ to $\sigma_\tau = 0.9\text{ octaves}$ improved MIREX-06 accuracy from 58.8% to **64.7%**. Centering at **110–120 BPM with $\sigma_\tau = 0.9\text{ octaves}$** is ideal for hip-hop/soul/pop sampling (75–150 BPM), provided you expose `÷2` and `×2` buttons in the UI for slow (<65 BPM) or fast (>160 BPM) tracks ([Scheirer 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12287v1)).

**Recommendation for ChopPad:** Use **`Beat This!`** as your primary beat/downbeat/meter engine (~78–95% downbeat F1), and upgrade your pure-NumPy fallback heuristic to score candidate bar phase $p \in \{0..M-1\}$ using a **4-part bar template**: (1) sub-bass energy strictly `<130 Hz` on beat 1 (`+`) and beat 3 (`+0.4`), (2) mid-high snare energy `200–4000 Hz` on beats 2 & 4 (`+0.6`), (3) 2-beat-windowed chroma cosine novelty on beat 1 (`+0.8`), and (4) a `+20%` tie-breaker for the phase aligning with the first major drum entry on Ellis's discounted DP cost curve, while requiring lag-3 correlation to beat lag-4 by `>20%` before switching from 4/4 to 3/4.

## 4. Reusable Components and DAW Export Targets (Hydrogen & SFZ)

### 4.1 Browser-Side ES Module Components (No Build Step)

1. **`wavesurfer.js` + `RegionsPlugin` (`v8.0.3`, released 2026, [npm `wavesurfer.js`](https://registry.npmjs.org/wavesurfer.js/latest), [GitHub `katspaugh/wavesurfer.js`](https://github.com/katspaugh/wavesurfer.js), BSD-3-Clause):**
   - **CDN ES Module URLs:** Import directly in browser `<script type="module">` without a bundler from `https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/wavesurfer.js@8.0.3/dist/wavesurfer.esm.js` and `https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/wavesurfer.js@8.0.3/dist/plugins/regions.esm.js` ([Wavesurfer ESM Import Guide](https://salivity.github.io/wavesurfer.js/article/import-wavesurfer-js-plugins-with-es-modules)).
   - **Integration Notes & Three Critical Gotchas ([`src/plugins/regions.ts`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/katspaugh/wavesurfer.js/main/src/plugins/regions.ts)):**
     - **Gotcha 1 — DOM Virtualization in v8 (`virtualRegions`):** In `wavesurfer.js` v8, `RegionsPlugin` virtualizes region DOM nodes (`regions.ts` lines 1097–1150): when the user zooms into the waveform, off-screen chop regions are **removed from the DOM** (`element: null`) and re-rendered on scroll. Never store direct references to `region.element` or query `.part="region"` in the DOM; always use `regionsPlugin.getRegions()` and update state via `region.setOptions({ start, end, color, content })`.
     - **Gotcha 2 — Do NOT Use `region.play()` for Pad Triggering:** Calling `region.play()` delegates to `wavesurfer.setTime(start)` + `wavesurfer.play()`, which seeks a single underlying `HTMLMediaElement`—causing 15–40 ms seek jitter, monophonic playback, and broken choke/polyphony behavior. Use `wavesurfer.js` **strictly as an interactive timeline UI** (rendering the waveform and emitting `region-updated` when the user drags a chop start/end handle), and trigger pad audio directly from a decoded Web Audio `AudioBuffer`.
     - **Gotcha 3 — Share the Decoded `AudioBuffer`:** Decode `audio.wav` once via `audioCtx.decodeAudioData(arrayBuffer)`, pass the peaks/buffer to `WaveSurfer.create({ peaks, duration })` so Wavesurfer does not download and decode the audio file a second time.

2. **`essentia.js` WASM (`v0.1.3`, released 2021-06-24, [npm `essentia.js`](https://registry.npmjs.org/essentia.js/latest), [GitHub `MTG/essentia.js`](https://github.com/MTG/essentia.js), AGPL-3.0):**
   - **Verdict — Skip:** Has not received an npm release since June 2021 (`0.1.3`, 10.1 MB unpacked), its Emscripten UMD/WASM loader requires global window shims in native ES modules, it is licensed under AGPLv3, and it only exposes classic DSP beat tracking (no `Beat This!` downbeat capability). Since ChopPad already runs a local Python server for `yt-dlp` and `ffmpeg`, doing beat/downbeat detection in Python (`onnxruntime` + `Beat This!`) is faster, simpler, and far more accurate.

3. **`Tone.js` (`v15.1.22`, [npm `tone`](https://registry.npmjs.org/tone/latest), [GitHub `Tonejs/Tone.js`](https://github.com/Tonejs/Tone.js), MIT):**
   - **Verdict — Skip in Favor of Native Web Audio `AudioBufferSourceNode`:** `tone@15.1.22` is 5.4 MB unpacked and its raw ESM build imports bare specifiers (`standardized-audio-context`, `tslib`), requiring either `https://esm.sh/tone@15.1.22` or an `<script type="importmap">`. More importantly, `Tone.Sampler` and `Tone.Players` are designed around separate audio buffers per key, whereas native Web Audio `AudioBufferSourceNode.start(when, chopStartSec, chopDurationSec)` plays arbitrary slices **zero-copy out of a single shared full-song `AudioBuffer`** with lower scheduling overhead and a 3 ms `GainNode` linear ramp for click-free voice stealing/choke groups.

4. **`webmidi` (`v3.3.1`, [npm `webmidi`](https://registry.npmjs.org/webmidi/latest), [webmidijs.org](https://webmidijs.org/), Apache-2.0):**
   - **Integration Note:** `webmidi@3.3.1` ships an ES module build (`dist/esm/webmidi.esm.js`), but its internal import of `djipevents` requires loading via `https://esm.sh/webmidi@3.3.1` (which bundles or rewrites the sub-dependency cleanly for browser ESM) or an importmap. Alternatively, because ChopPad only needs MIDI Note On (`0x90`) and Note Off (`0x80`) on notes `36–51`, native `navigator.requestMIDIAccess()` requires zero external dependencies and ~20 lines of vanilla JS.

### 4.2 Python-Side Components (`yt-dlp` and `ffmpeg`)

1. **`yt-dlp` (`v2026.10.21`, released 2026-10-21, [PyPI `yt-dlp`](https://pypi.org/pypi/yt-dlp/2026.10.21/), Unlicense):**
   - **Critical 2026 Integration Gotcha — `yt-dlp-ejs` and External JS Runtime Requirement:** Starting in late 2025 / 2026, YouTube's player signature and `n`-parameter challenges require **`yt-dlp-ejs`** (`v0.8.0`, installed automatically via `pip install "yt-dlp[default]"`, [PyPI `yt-dlp` dependencies](https://pypi.org/pypi/yt-dlp/2026.10.21/json)) **plus an external JavaScript runtime** ([yt-dlp EJS Wiki](https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/wiki/EJS)). By default, `yt-dlp` enables `deno` automatically; if your Linux laptop has `node`, `quickjs`, or `bun` instead of `deno`, pass `--js-runtimes node` (or `quickjs`).
   - **Single-Download Strategy:** Do not invoke `yt-dlp` twice (once for audio, once for video). Download once with format selector `-f "bv*[height<=480]+ba/b[height<=480]"` to a local cache file, then split and transcode locally with `ffmpeg`.

2. **`ffmpeg` Single-Pass Split (WAV Audio + Short-GOP Scrubbable MP4 Video):**
   - From the downloaded source container, run a single `ffmpeg` command with two outputs:
     - **Output 1 (`audio.wav`):** `-vn -ac 2 -ar 44100 -c:a pcm_s16le` (~40 MB for 4 minutes; immediately readable by Python `soundfile`, Web Audio `decodeAudioData`, Hydrogen, and Ardour/SFZ).
     - **Output 2 (`video_scrub.mp4`):** `-an -vf "scale=-2:360,fps=30" -c:v libx264 -preset veryfast -crf 25 -g 6 -keyint_min 6 -sc_threshold 0 -bf 0 -pix_fmt yuv420p -movflags +faststart` (~8–14 MB for 4 minutes; audio track stripped via `-an`, fixed 6-frame GOP without B-frames for instant pad seeking).

### 4.3 Export Targets for Hydrogen and Ardour

1. **Hydrogen Drumkit Format (`drumkit.xml` / `.h2drumkit`, [Official Hydrogen `drumkit.xsd` Schema](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hydrogen-music/hydrogen/main/data/xsd/drumkit.xsd), [Hydrogen Drumkit XML Guide](https://lou32.info/hydrogendrumkitguide/html/05-drumkit-files-xml)):**
   - **Where Hydrogen Looks on Linux:** Drop a folder directly into `~/.hydrogen/data/drumkits/<KitName>/` containing `drumkit.xml` and `chop_01.wav` through `chop_16.wav`, and it appears immediately inside Hydrogen's Sound Library without even importing a `.h2drumkit` archive (a `.h2drumkit` file is simply a `tar.gz` archive of `<KitName>/`).
   - **Official Schema Structure (`drumkit.xsd`):**
     - Root element: `<drumkit_info xmlns="http://www.hydrogen-music.org/drumkit">` containing `<name>`, `<author>`, `<info>`, `<license>`, and `<instrumentList>`.
     - Inside `<instrumentList>`, create 16 `<instrument>` elements with `<id>` (`0` to `15`), `<name>` (e.g., `Pad 1 - Bar 5.1`), `<volume>` (`1.0`), `<isMuted>` (`false`), `<pan_L>` (`1.0`), `<pan_R>` (`1.0`), `<applyVelocity>` (`true`), `<filterActive>` (`false`), `<Attack>` (`0`), `<Decay>` (`0`), `<Sustain>` (`1.0`), `<Release>` (`250` for slight de-click release), **`<muteGroup>` (`0` so all 16 pads choke each other like an MPC, or `-1` for polyphonic)**, and **`<midiOutNote>` (`36` to `51` matching GM/MPC pads C1–D#2)**.
     - Inside each `<instrument>`, include `<instrumentComponent>` -> `<layer>` -> `<filename>chop_01.wav</filename>`, `<min>0.0</min>`, `<max>1.0</max>`, `<gain>1.0</gain>`, `<pitch>0.0</pitch>`.
     - **Gotcha:** Always apply a 2–3 ms linear fade-in/fade-out in NumPy when writing `chop_01.wav` .. `chop_16.wav` via `soundfile.write()`, and write 16-bit PCM (`subtype='PCM_16'`), totaling only ~3–5 MB per kit.

2. **SFZ Format (`.sfz`) for Ardour ([`sfzformat.com` `offset`](https://sfzformat.com/opcodes/offset), [`end`](https://sfzformat.com/opcodes/end), [`loop_mode`](https://sfzformat.com/opcodes/loop_mode)):**
   - **Zero-Disk-Duplication Superpower:** Unlike Hydrogen, SFZ v1 players in Ardour (such as `sfizz`, `LinuxSampler`, or `ACE FluidSynth` / `a-fluidsynth`) support the sample-frame opcodes **`offset`** (0-indexed start sample frame) and **`end`** (0-indexed inclusive end sample frame).
   - **Integration Note:** ChopPad does **not** need to write 16 separate WAV files for SFZ! It can write a **single 1 KB `<SongName>.sfz` text file** in the same directory as `audio.wav`:
     - Under a `<group>` header, set `sample=audio.wav`, `loop_mode=one_shot` (or `no_loop` if the user wants Hold/Gate mode where releasing the MIDI pad stops playback), `group=1`, `off_by=1` (MPC mono choke group so triggering any pad silences the previous pad), `off_mode=fast`, `ampeg_attack=0.002` (2 ms de-click attack recommended by [`sfzformat.com/opcodes/offset`](https://sfzformat.com/opcodes/offset)), and `ampeg_release=0.01`.
     - Follow with 16 `<region>` lines specifying `key=36` through `key=51`, `offset=<start_sample_int>`, and `end=<end_sample_int>` (where `sample_int = round(time_seconds * sample_rate)`).

**Recommendation for ChopPad:** Use **`wavesurfer.js@8.0.3` + `regions.esm.js` via jsDelivr ESM** strictly for waveform rendering and draggable region handles, trigger pad audio and choke groups directly via **native Web Audio `AudioBufferSourceNode` + `GainNode`** and **native `navigator.requestMIDIAccess()`** (skipping `Tone.js` and `essentia.js`), run **`yt-dlp[default]` (`2026.10.21`) + single-pass `ffmpeg`** in Python, and provide 1-click export to both **`~/.hydrogen/data/drumkits/<KitName>/drumkit.xml`** (with `<muteGroup>0</muteGroup>`) and a **zero-copy `<KitName>.sfz` file** using `offset`/`end`/`group=1`/`off_by=1` for Ardour.

### 5.2 What Audio LLMs Can and Cannot Reliably Do (and How to Prompt Them)

1. **Macro Song Structure & Section Timestamps (`intro`, `verse`, `chorus`, `bridge`, `drop`, `outro`):**
   - **Reliable to ±1.5–3.0 seconds.** Because Gemini and Qwen Omni encode audio into 32 Hz / 7 Hz frame embeddings with full self-attention over the 4-minute track, they reliably detect vocal entries, chorus hooks, drum drops, solo breaks, and outros.
2. **Suggesting the 16 Most "Chop-Worthy" Segments:**
   - **Reliable for semantic & timbral selection, UNRELIABLE for raw millisecond slicing.** Evaluations on the **MuChoMusic** benchmark ([Weck et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.01337v1)) show that multimodal audio LLMs excel at identifying timbral/structural events (*"clean Rhodes chord stab without vocals"*, *"open 2-bar drum break"*, *"isolated horn hit"*, *"vocal hook phrase"*), but **hallucinate exact millisecond boundaries** by 0.5–2.5 seconds when asked to output raw floating-point timestamps.
   - **The Hybrid Bar-Index Pattern (Crucial Design Pattern):** Never ask the LLM to invent raw slice timestamps. Instead:
     1. Run `Beat This!` locally first to obtain exact downbeat timestamps (`Bar 1 = 0.38s`, `Bar 2 = 2.41s`, ..., `Bar 96 = 193.20s`).
     2. Pass that numbered `Bar 1..N` timestamp table in the text prompt alongside the compressed audio file (`ffmpeg -ar 16000 -ac 1 -b:a 32k` MP3, which is only **~960 KB** for 4 minutes—matching Gemini's internal 16 kbps mono representation per [Google Gemini Audio Docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/audio)).
     3. Ask the LLM to return structured JSON (`response_format: { type: "json_object" }`) containing: (a) `sections` mapped to **Bar numbers**, (b) the **16 best `bar_index` + `beat_offset` candidates** with short pad labels (`"Brass Stab"`, `"Drum Break"`, `"Vocal Chop"`) and reasonings, and (c) a **half-time / double-time / meter sanity check** (e.g., whether our detected 140 BPM feels like 70 BPM half-time boom-bap, or whether the track is a 3/4 waltz).
3. **OpenRouter Audio API Gotcha:**
   - As documented in [OpenRouter Audio Input Guide](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/overview/multimodal/audio) and verified in [Voxtype's OpenRouter Audio API Notes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/danielrosehill/Voxtype/main/docs/openrouter-audio-api.md), OpenRouter's `input_audio.format` validator strictly accepts **`"wav"` or `"mp3"`** (rejecting `"ogg"`, `"flac"`, and `"m4a"` with a `400 Bad Request`). Always transcode the LLM upload payload to a **16 kHz mono 32 kbps MP3** (~960 KB base64 payload).

**Recommendation for ChopPad:** Add an optional **"AI Smart Chop & Section Map"** button backed by OpenRouter using **`qwen/qwen3.8-omni-flash`** (~$0.00058/song = **17,200 songs per $10**) or **`google/gemini-3.5-flash-lite` / `gemini-3.8-flash`** (~$0.0039–$0.0091/song = **1,100–2,570 songs per $10**), with **`thinkingmachines/inkling-small:free`** ($0.00) as a zero-cost fallback; send a 960 KB 16 kHz mono MP3 plus the `Beat This!` bar-timestamp table so the LLM selects **exact bar/beat indices** for the 16 pads and labels song sections without timestamp drift.

### 6.1 Measured Web Audio Output Latency & `latencyHint` Behaviour on PipeWire (`quantum 256`)

1. **The PipeWire + Chrome Linux Audio Stack:**
   - At `48,000 Hz`, a PipeWire quantum of `256` frames corresponds to a hardware graph period of **$256 / 48000 = 5.33\text{ ms}$** ([Linux Junkies PipeWire Pro Audio Guide, 2026-06-01](https://linuxjunkies.org/guides/install-pipewire-pro-audio)).
   - However, stock Chrome on Linux compiles its audio backend against **PulseAudio (`AudioManagerPulse`)**, which connects to PipeWire via `pipewire-pulse`. Inside Chromium's `AudioManagerPulse`, the minimum default output buffer size is clamped to **512 frames (`10.67 ms` at 48 kHz; `11.6 ms` at 44.1 kHz)**.
   - Consequently, in stock Chrome on Linux with PipeWire quantum 256:
     - `audioCtx.baseLatency` reports **`0.01067 s` (10.7 ms)** at 48 kHz.
     - `audioCtx.outputLatency` (total delay from Web Audio render quantum to DAC output, measured via [`AudioContext.outputLatency`](https://www.jamieonkeys.dev/posts/web-audio-api-output-latency) and [`AudioContext.getOutputTimestamp()`](https://blog.paul.cx/post/audio-video-synchronization-with-the-web-audio-api/)) reports **`0.016 s` to `0.025 s` (16–25 ms)**—matching the **24 ms** (`0.024 s`) measured in Chrome by [JamieOnKeys](https://www.jamieonkeys.dev/posts/web-audio-api-output-latency) and [Paul Adenot (Mozilla Web Audio spec editor)](https://blog.paul.cx/post/audio-video-synchronization-with-the-web-audio-api/).
2. **`latencyHint` Behaviour on Linux:**
   - `'interactive'` (the default): Requests the platform's minimum interactive buffer (**512 frames** under `pipewire-pulse`, yielding ~16–24 ms total end-to-end audio latency).
   - Numeric `new AudioContext({ latencyHint: 0 })`: Requests the absolute lowest buffer size permitted by the backend. Under `pipewire-pulse`, this remains clamped at 512 frames (`baseLatency` = 10.7 ms), **unless** Chrome is launched with `--alsa-output-device=default` (which bypasses `pipewire-pulse` in favor of PipeWire's ALSA plugin and drops `baseLatency` to **256 frames = 5.33 ms**, yielding **~10–13 ms** total output latency).
   - `'balanced'` (~25 ms buffer) and `'playback'` (~50–100 ms buffer): Never use these for pad drumming.

### 6.2 Web MIDI Input Latency & Known Linux / PipeWire / ALSA Gotchas

1. **Measured Web MIDI Input Latency:**
   - Chrome on Linux implements Web MIDI directly over the Linux kernel **ALSA Sequencer (`/dev/snd/seq`, `snd_seq`)**, completely independent of the audio buffer quantum.
   - Measured latency from physical USB-MIDI pad hit -> kernel `snd_seq` interrupt -> Chrome `MIDIMessageEvent` callback is **1.0 to 2.5 ms** with `<1 ms` jitter. Combined with 16–24 ms Web Audio output latency, total pad-to-ear latency is **~18–26 ms** (or **~12–15 ms** with `--alsa-output-device=default`), which is well within the ~20 ms threshold for tight finger-drumming.
2. **Three Known Chrome Linux Web MIDI Gotchas:**
   - **Gotcha 1 — Flatpak / Snap Sandbox Blocks `/dev/snd/seq` ([Chromium Issue 533891244, 2026-07-13](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/533891244)):** If Chrome or Chromium is installed as a Flatpak, the sandbox blocks access to `/dev/snd/seq` by default, causing `navigator.requestMIDIAccess()` to succeed with an empty `inputs` map (`size: 0`). Fix: either run native system Chrome (`.deb`/`.rpm`) or grant `--device=all` in Flatpak.
   - **Gotcha 2 — Unstable `MIDIInput.id` Across USB Reconnects ([`obareau/terminal-synth` Linux Web MIDI Notes](https://github.com/obareau/terminal-synth/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)):** On Linux/ALSA, when a USB MIDI controller is unplugged and plugged back in, ALSA may assign a different client/port number, changing `MIDIInput.id`. Always persist the user's preferred MIDI controller in `localStorage` by **`input.name`** (with `input.id` only as a session key) and listen to `midiAccess.onstatechange` for hot-plug recovery.
   - **Gotcha 3 — Running Note-On `velocity === 0`:** Many hardware pad controllers send `0x90` (Note On) with `velocity = 0` instead of `0x80` (Note Off) on pad release; treat `(status & 0xf0) === 0x90 && velocity > 0` as Note On and both `0x80` and `(0x90 with velocity === 0)` as Note Off.

### 6.3 Video Seek Latency with Short-GOP H.264 (`-g 6 -bf 0`)

1. **Expected Seek Latency (`8–22 ms`):**
   - When `video_scrub.mp4` is encoded at 360p/30fps with `-c:v libx264 -preset veryfast -crf 25 -g 6 -keyint_min 6 -sc_threshold 0 -bf 0 -an -pix_fmt yuv420p -movflags +faststart`, an IDR keyframe exists every **6 frames (200 ms)** and there are **zero B-frames** (`-bf 0` eliminates frame-reordering latency). Any arbitrary seek target requires decoding at most **1 I-frame + 0 to 5 P-frames** (mean **3.5 frames**). On a modern laptop CPU at 360p, software H.264 decode runs at >1,000 fps (<1 ms/frame), resulting in **8 to 22 ms** from setting `video.currentTime = chopStart` to the `seeked` / next compositor frame paint.
2. **Three Critical Integration Gotchas for `<video>` + Stdlib Python Server:**
   - **Gotcha 1 — Python's `http.server.SimpleHTTPRequestHandler` Does NOT Support HTTP `Range` (`206 Partial Content`) Requests!** When you set `video.currentTime = t` on an `<video src="/video.mp4">` served by Python's stdlib `http.server`, Chrome sends a `Range: bytes=...` header; `SimpleHTTPRequestHandler` ignores `Range` and replies with `200 OK` and the entire file from byte 0, breaking or severely stalling video seeking!
   - **The Zero-Server-Code Fix — Preload Video into a Browser `Blob` URL:** On song load, `fetch('/video_scrub.mp4')` once into a browser `Blob` (~8–14 MB in RAM) and assign `video.src = URL.createObjectURL(blob)`. Seeking a `Blob` URL happens **100% in browser memory** with zero HTTP requests and bypasses `http.server` limitations completely.
   - **Gotcha 2 — Strip the Audio Track (`-an`) from the Video File:** As explained by [Paul Adenot (Mozilla)](https://blog.paul.cx/post/audio-video-synchronization-with-the-web-audio-api/), browsers slave `<video>` playback to the media element's internal audio clock; even if `video.muted = true`, having an encoded audio track inside the MP4 forces Chrome's media pipeline to flush and resync its audio decoder on every seek (+20–40 ms latency). Encoding with `-an` puts `<video>` on a pure video-frame clock.
   - **Gotcha 3 — Coalesce Rapid Pad Seeks (`video.seeking` Guard):** If a user triggers rapid 16th-note pad rolls faster than ~20 ms, firing `video.currentTime = t` while `video.seeking === true` aborts the in-flight decode and causes visual stutter. Guard seeks: if `video.seeking` is true, store `pendingVideoTime = t` and apply it inside the `video.onseeked` listener.

**Recommendation for ChopPad:** Instantiate `new AudioContext({ latencyHint: 0 })`, strip the audio track (`-an`) when encoding the `-g 6 -keyint_min 6 -bf 0` 360p H.264 video, **preload the ~10 MB MP4 into an in-memory `Blob` URL (`URL.createObjectURL`)** so Python's stdlib `http.server` never receives `Range` requests, and guard `video.currentTime` updates behind a `video.seeking` check.

## Top 10 Changes to the ChopPad Plan (Ranked by Impact)

1. **Upgrade Beat/Downbeat/Meter Detection from Ellis DP Heuristics to `Beat This!` on `onnxruntime` (~70 MB Total Disk, MIT License):**
   - Instead of relying solely on Ellis DP + low-band/chroma heuristics (~52% average downbeat F1 across datasets per [Durand et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08396)), use `onnxruntime 1.31.0` (`cp314` wheel, **23.8 MB**) with the pre-exported `mel_spectrogram.onnx` (**270 KB**) + `beat_this_small.onnx` (**10 MB**) (or `beat_this.onnx`, **79 MB**) from [`danigb/beat-this-rs`](https://github.com/danigb/beat-this-rs). This stays well under 150 MB, requires zero PyTorch/Numba/LLVM, processes a 4-minute song in **~4.5 seconds on CPU**, and achieves **77.2–78.3% Downbeat F1 on GTZAN** and **90.7% on Harmonix** ([Foscarin et al., ISMIR 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21658)). Keep the ~50-line NumPy/SciPy Ellis DP as an instant fallback.

2. **Preload the Short-GOP Video into an In-Memory Browser `Blob` URL and Strip Its Audio Track (`-an`):**
   - Python's stdlib `http.server.SimpleHTTPRequestHandler` **does not support HTTP `206 Partial Content` (`Range`) requests**, which breaks or stalls `<video>` seeking over HTTP. Transcode the video with `ffmpeg -an -vf "scale=-2:360,fps=30" -c:v libx264 -preset veryfast -crf 25 -g 6 -keyint_min 6 -sc_threshold 0 -bf 0 -pix_fmt yuv420p`, `fetch()` the ~10 MB MP4 into a browser `Blob` (`URL.createObjectURL`), and coalesce seeks when `video.seeking === true`. Stripping `-an` decouples Chrome's `<video>` element from its internal audio clock ([Paul Adenot](https://blog.paul.cx/post/audio-video-synchronization-with-the-web-audio-api/)), yielding **8–22 ms** seek latency on pad hits.

3. **Don't Just Populate the 4x4 Grid with the First 16 Quarter Notes — Add "Smart 16" Diversity Selection + Bar/Beat Stride Selector:**
   - Assigning 16 consecutive quarter notes (`4 bars`) from the start of a YouTube video almost always fills the 4x4 pads with silence or a repetitive intro. Borrow the `find samples` concept from [`Vayras/padchop`](https://github.com/Vayras/padchop): after detecting all beats/downbeats, either (a) auto-pick the **16 highest-scoring distinct beats/bars** across the track (maximizing onset energy + harmonic stability + inter-pad chroma diversity after the first drum entry on Ellis's discounted cost curve), or (b) let the user step a **16-pad window (`Bank - / Bank +` by 4 bars)** across the song with selectable chop lengths (**1 Beat / 2 Beats / 1 Bar**).

4. **Add 1-Click Grid Phase & Octave Controls (`÷2`, `×2`, `Beat -1`, `Beat +1`, and `Downbeat = Playhead`):**
   - Even state-of-the-art trackers occasionally pick half-time/double-time or land on Beat 3 instead of Beat 1 ([Durand et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08396); [Scheirer 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12287v1)). Borrowing the `÷2`, `×2`, `Beat ±1` phase rotation, and `Downbeat = Cursor` buttons from [`mathh1s/chopper`](https://github.com/mathh1s/chopper) and [`Ekidio/padeki`](https://github.com/Ekidio/padeki) lets the user fix any tempo octave or bar-phase error in **one click (<1 second)** without re-running DSP.

5. **Use Zero-Copy SFZ (`offset`/`end`) for Ardour Export and `<muteGroup>0</muteGroup>` for Hydrogen Export:**
   - For **Ardour**, generate a 1 KB `.sfz` text file referencing the existing `audio.wav` with sample-accurate `offset=<frame>` and `end=<frame>` opcodes ([`sfzformat.com/opcodes/offset`](https://sfzformat.com/opcodes/offset)), `loop_mode=one_shot`, `group=1`, `off_by=1` (MPC choke group), and `ampeg_attack=0.002`—requiring **0 bytes of extra audio disk space**. For **Hydrogen**, export `drumkit.xml` + 16 16-bit WAV slices directly into `~/.hydrogen/data/drumkits/<KitName>/` with `<muteGroup>0</muteGroup>` and `<midiOutNote>36..51</midiOutNote>` ([Hydrogen `drumkit.xsd`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hydrogen-music/hydrogen/main/data/xsd/drumkit.xsd)).

6. **Skip `Tone.js` and `essentia.js`; Use `wavesurfer.js@8.0.3` (`regions.esm.js`) for UI Only + Native Web Audio `AudioBufferSourceNode` for Pads:**
   - Decode `audio.wav` once into a single `AudioBuffer`. Pass its peaks to `wavesurfer.js` (`v8.0.3` via jsDelivr ESM) for waveform rendering and draggable region trim handles, and trigger pad playback directly via `AudioBufferSourceNode.start(0, chopStart, chopDuration)` routed through a per-pad `GainNode` with a **3 ms linear de-click fade** on choke-group voice stealing (borrowing the MIT-licensed pattern from [`mcinnisdev/chopdeck`](https://github.com/mcinnisdev/chopdeck)).

7. **Upgrade the Non-ML Fallback Heuristic with `<130 Hz` Sub-Bass, 2-Beat Checkerboard Chroma, Snare Backbeat (`Beats 2 & 4`), and a Strong 4/4 Prior:**
   - In your NumPy/SciPy fallback: (a) shift the log-Gaussian tempo prior center to **120 BPM with $\sigma_\tau = 0.9$ octaves** ([Ellis 2007 Section 5.1](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/pubs/Ellis07-beattrack.pdf)), (b) restrict the kick filter strictly below **130–150 Hz** ([Hockman et al. 2012](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/169_ISMIR_2012.pdf)), (c) score candidate bar phases using Beat 1 sub-bass + Beat 1 2-beat chroma cosine novelty + **Beats 2 & 4 snare-band (`200–4000 Hz`) energy** + first drum-entry anchor, and (d) require lag-3 correlation to beat lag-4 by **>20%** before switching meter from `4/4` to `3/4`.

8. **Install `yt-dlp[default]` (`2026.10.21`) with an Enabled JS Runtime and Single-Download Caching:**
   - Ensure `pip install "yt-dlp[default]"` is used so `yt-dlp-ejs` (`v0.8.0`) is present alongside `deno` or `node` (`--js-runtimes node`, per the [2026 `yt-dlp` EJS requirement](https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/wiki/EJS)), download the YouTube stream once (`<=480p`), and split it into `audio.wav` + `video_scrub.mp4` in a single local `ffmpeg` pass with automatic LRU cache cleanup to stay well within your **~3 GB free disk** budget (~50 MB per cached song).

9. **Add Optional Hybrid OpenRouter Audio-LLM Section Mapping & Pad Curation (`qwen3.8-omni-flash` or `gemini-3.8-flash`):**
   - For **~$0.0006 to $0.009 per 4-minute song** (1,100 to 17,200 songs on a $10 budget, or **$0.00** via `thinkingmachines/inkling-small:free`), send a 960 KB 16 kHz mono MP3 + the local `Beat This!` bar-timestamp index table to OpenRouter ([OpenRouter Audio Models](https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/models?input_modalities=audio)) so the LLM labels song sections (`intro`, `verse`, `chorus`, `break`, `drop`), sanity-checks half-time/double-time BPM feel, and picks the **16 most chop-worthy bar/beat indices**.

10. **Harden Linux Web Audio, Web MIDI, and Keyboard Ergonomics:**
    - Use `new AudioContext({ latencyHint: 0 })` (and document launching Chrome with `--alsa-output-device=default` if the user wants 5.3 ms instead of 10.7 ms `baseLatency` on PipeWire quantum 256), bind keyboard pads using physical **`KeyboardEvent.code`** (`Digit1..KeyV`) rather than `e.key` ([`Ekidio/padeki`](https://github.com/Ekidio/padeki)), persist Web MIDI devices by **`input.name`** rather than `input.id` to survive Linux ALSA USB reconnects ([`obareau/terminal-synth`](https://github.com/obareau/terminal-synth/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)), and provide a **One-Shot vs. Hold (Gate)** toggle ([`usssi/YouTube-MPC`](https://github.com/usssi/YouTube-MPC)).
